The actual problem

Style advice is written for people who already have style.

Ask the internet what suits you and you get vibes. Wear what makes you feel confident. Find your aesthetic. Invest in quality basics. None of it tells you which basics, in which colour, cut how, for your face and your frame.

The irritating part is that a lot of it is measurable. Whether a colour flatters your face is a question about the distance between two points in a perceptual colour space. Whether a hem length works on you is a question about where it falls between your shoulder and the floor. Those have answers. This finds them and shows its working.

Some of this is measurement and some is judgement. The page says which is which, every time, and the score is labelled a confidence rather than dressed up as a fact.

Why it works

The colour maths

Why RGB is the wrong place to do this.

Two pairs of colours can sit the same numeric distance apart in RGB and look nothing alike. Everything here happens in CIELAB instead, which was built so that equal numeric distance means roughly equal perceived difference.

Try it

Move either colour and watch the two numbers disagree. RGB distance is the naive answer; ΔE2000 is the one that matches what your eye reports.

Depth is measured, not guessed

The Individual Typology Angle is the figure dermatology uses to classify skin objectively: the angle of your skin's position on the lightness-against-yellowness plane, ITA° = arctan((L*−50) / b*) × 180/π. Six bands, from very light through to deep, with thresholds published by Chardon and later extended by Del Bino. It separates depth far more cleanly than lightness on its own, and it is not a judgement anyone made up.

Undertone has physiology underneath it

Two pigments pull your skin in different directions: melanin pushes the yellow axis up, haemoglobin pushes the red axis up. The hue angle h = atan2(b*, a*) is exactly the balance between them — high is melanin-led and reads golden, low is haemoglobin-led and reads rosy. That is why this asks a camera rather than asking whether silver or gold jewellery suits you.

The washed-out colour can be derived

A colour within a few degrees of your skin's hue and a few points of its lightness has nothing to push against, so worn near the face it reads as an extension of it. That is the whole mechanism behind "that colour washes you out", and it is the one piece of colour advice that is genuinely computable. So it gets computed, and shown to you as a swatch.

The implementation is checked against the Sharma, Wu and Dalal test vectors — the set used to certify CIEDE2000 implementations — and against a Python reference implementation across three hundred colours, to nine decimal places.

The proportion maths

There is exactly one length that always looks wrong.

Sources disagree about which uneven proportion is ideal — a third, two thirds, the golden section. They agree on the negative: a hem that splits your silhouette into two equal halves is the one that reliably reads badly. That line is computable from your height, so this computes it instead of inventing a precision nobody has.

Landmarks, not guesses

Shoulder at 0.818 of your height, natural waist at 0.600, hip joint at 0.530, knee at 0.285. These are the standard body-segment ratios used in biomechanics. They are population averages, which is why the page says so every time it leans on one.

Volume is a budget

"Do baggy clothes suit me" has no yes or no. Nearly everyone can wear volume; very few can wear it everywhere at once, because two voluminous garments stop reading as a silhouette and start reading as a pile. So each garment has a cost and each frame has an allowance.

Drop is the tailor's own number

Chest minus waist. A drop of six is a regular cut, eight is athletic, four or less is a fuller cut. It is printed on the inside of half the jackets in any department store, and knowing yours is the difference between guessing a size and choosing one.

Honestly

What this won't do.

It won't show you real product photographs

Every image of a garment online belongs to whoever shot it. A styling tool that hotlinks other people's lookbooks is a copyright problem with a colour picker attached. So the pieces are drawn as vectors in the exact colour the engine chose — which is more honest anyway, because the swatch on screen is the recommendation rather than an approximation of it.

It won't generate a picture of you wearing it

Generating photorealistic images of a real person from their photo is a line this deliberately does not cross, and the photo never leaves your device in any case.

It won't pretend the score is a measurement

There is no ground truth for "does this outfit suit him". The match figure is the model showing how well an outfit satisfies its own stated rules. The colour distances underneath it are real measurements; the weighting of them is a judgement, and it is written down in the source rather than hidden.

It won't read a bad photo well

Heavy colour filters, strong coloured light, or a face in deep shadow will move the reading. The illuminant correction handles ordinary indoor warmth, not a neon sign. When the automatic read looks wrong, drag the sample points — you can see your own face better than any heuristic can.

It won't tell you where to buy any of it

Not yet, and not dishonestly. Retail data goes stale fast — over the course of building this, one chain closed, a listing sold between being indexed and being checked, and a whole category went out of stock inside ten minutes. Shopping links belong on a scheduled feed from retailers who publish one, not on a live scrape pretending to be current.

It won't decide anything for you

Taste is still yours. This narrows an infinite problem down to three defensible answers and explains each one. If you disagree with all three, the explanations should at least tell you what to change.
